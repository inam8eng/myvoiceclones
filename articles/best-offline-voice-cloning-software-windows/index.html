You want to clone your own voice, and you do not want to upload your recordings to a server or pay a monthly fee forever. The short answer: if you want a free tool and you do not mind doing some setup, start with Voicebox or VoiceStudio. If you mostly convert singing or recorded vocals, check out Xport Studio. What does "offline" really mean for these apps?

None of these programs works offline on the very first day. Each one needs to download a large AI model once, and after that file is on your drive, the cloning and speech generation happen on your computer. Xport Studio states that its voice pack is about 4.7 GB, and my own app downloads an engine of about 1.3 GB when you run it the first time.

Once that initial download finishes, the real difference is what still connects to the internet. VoiceStudio states that its free app goes online only when you tell it to check for an update or download a new model, whereas its paid Pro build requires an account and sends licence and usage data. VoiceClone verifies a licence key online one time and runs offline after that. So the honest test is simple: finish the setup, turn your Wi-Fi off, and clone and generate something. If it still works, it is offline.

How do these apps clone a voice, and why does it matter?

A local app can copy a voice in two main ways, and each method fits a different kind of user. The first method is an instant clone, which engineers call zero-shot cloning, meaning the model copies a voice on the spot without being trained on it first. You feed the app a few seconds of someone talking, and it mimics that voice right away. Voicebox says it can clone from as little as 3 seconds of audio, and Voice Creator Pro says 3 to 10 seconds.

The second method is a trained clone, where the software studies your voice and saves what it learns as a permanent file on your disk. You record several minutes of clean speech, the app trains a model on those recordings, and you keep the resulting file. Xport Studio asks for 5 to 30 minutes of clean vocals, and VoiceClone trains a GPT-SoVITS model from a few minutes of your normal talking. Training takes more time, but the output usually keeps your accent and natural rhythm steady across a long script. I covered this difference in detail in instant vs professional voice cloning.

Xport Studio also offers a third approach called voice conversion, which works differently from reading typed text aloud. You sing or speak a line into your microphone, and the app transforms your recording into the trained voice, which is how RVC tools operate. That is a completely different workflow from typing a script and generating speech. If you want to see how these two engines compare, I wrote about RVC vs GPT-SoVITS.

Which free offline voice cloner should you try first?

Voicebox is the easiest free option for most people to start with. It is open source under the MIT licence, comes with a Windows installer, and lets you choose between 7 speech engines, including Qwen3-TTS, Chatterbox and Kokoro. Its pricing page states that the app is free and only an optional cloud backup will carry a fee, listed at $12 a year and marked coming soon. It can generate up to 50,000 characters in a single run and works on NVIDIA, AMD and Intel graphics cards.

VoiceStudio is worth trying if you work in many languages or need to dub videos. It states that it supports 646 languages, it is open source under AGPL-3.0, and it notes that about 8 GB of RAM is enough to get started, with a graphics card simply speeding things up. A paid Pro build is listed at $99 a year or $199 on its Pro page, and it was marked coming soon in the site's own comparison table when I looked.

There is one thing I want to point out about Voicebox. Its homepage demo displays voice profiles named after famous people, and both Voicebox and VoiceStudio can technically clone whatever audio you give them. That does not make it allowed. Clone your own voice, or a voice whose owner said yes in writing. VoiceStudio states this same rule in its own FAQ.

Can you use the cloned voice for paid work?

Licensing is where many people get tripped up, because two separate licences apply instead of one. The desktop app has its own licence, and the AI model running inside that app has a separate licence, so whichever rule is stricter is the one you must follow. VoiceStudio states this clearly on its homepage: paying for its Pro build does not override the model's terms, and it points out that the OmniVoice pretrained model is noncommercial.

When an app advertises full commercial rights, check which underlying model you are using before you sell anything created with it. If you look at the raw models themselves, the terms are straightforward. MIT and Apache 2.0 licences permit paid work, so Qwen3-TTS and VoxCPM2 are safe to use commercially. A CC BY-NC licence prohibits paid work, so F5-TTS is free to test but cannot be used for commercial sales.

If you plan to earn from the voice, check the model's licence, not only the app's. Regardless of what the software licence permits, the voice itself must still belong to you or to someone who gave you permission to use it.

Which paid offline apps are worth it?

Xport Studio is designed first and foremost as a music and vocals studio. Its voice cloning feature comes with a 7 day free trial, and after that it is included in Xport Pro for $79 one time with no recurring subscription. It runs on Windows 10 or later and on Apple Silicon Macs running macOS 15 or later. The software also includes stem splitting and a text to speech mode that reads typed lines and converts them into your trained voice, making it a better fit for singers and music producers than for standard narrators.

Voice Creator Pro offers a desktop lifetime licence starting at $54.99 one time with unlimited generations, alongside cloud subscription plans that begin at $5 a month. Its desktop interface brings several models together in one window so you can switch engines depending on the task. That setup is handy when you want to test different engines side by side, and it also means the model licence rule from the previous section applies to whichever engine you choose.

FAQ

Is there free voice cloning software that works offline on Windows?

Yes. Voicebox and VoiceStudio are both free, open source and run on Windows, and once you finish the one time model download, they work without an internet connection. VoiceClone also offers a free version that lets you clone and fully train one voice and create 3 generations.

Do offline voice cloning apps need a graphics card?

Most of these apps can run on a standard PC, just at a slower speed. VoiceStudio notes that about 8 GB of RAM is enough to start, and VoiceClone runs on a regular processor as well, though a graphics card makes both programs much faster. Training a voice model is the stage where a graphics card makes the biggest difference.

Can I use offline voice cloning software for YouTube or client work?

In most cases yes, provided two conditions are met. First, the AI model inside the app must carry a licence that permits commercial use, such as MIT or Apache 2.0, and second, the voice must belong to you or to someone who gave you written permission.

Which offline voice cloner works on both Mac and Windows?

Voicebox, VoiceStudio, Xport Studio and Voice Creator Pro all offer Mac versions alongside Windows. VoiceClone runs only on Windows 10 and 11, so if you use a Mac, pick one of the other four.
